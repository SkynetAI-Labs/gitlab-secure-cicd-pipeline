# GitLab Lab 03 — Enterprise Buyers, Stakeholders & Buying Process

**Draft outline · Independent Portfolio Project · Adam / SkynetAI-Labs**

Audience: GitLab's sales hiring team. This is a proposed next lab, not a completed enterprise engagement, buyer interview, opportunity, or hiring-team assessment. Public handbook sources checked 25 October 2026. GitHub's separate technical Lab 03 is unrelated and remains unchanged.

## Purpose and distinct contribution

Show how I would understand an enterprise buying group, test access to decision authority, coordinate stakeholders, and distinguish a technical recommendation from a funded, executable purchase. The central interview question is: **“The technical team likes the solution; what still has to happen for this organisation to buy and adopt it?”**

Lab 01 supplies technical evidence. Lab 02 supplies the fair evaluation and discovery toolkit. Lab 03 would explain who needs to agree, how their priorities interact, what each approval requires, and what evidence justifies the next step. The structure below is my proposed portfolio design, informed by GitLab's public sales handbook; it is not an official hiring rubric or a universal customer process.

## 1. Enterprise buyer hypotheses

Use a fictional, unnamed organisation with multiple engineering teams and a possible release-evidence standardisation initiative. Size, budget, business units, existing contracts, urgency, and governance remain unknown. Start from roles and responsibilities; a senior title is not proof of budget authority.

| Role hypothesis | What a future persona card should explore | Evidence needed to validate the buying role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic decision authority / executive sponsor | Business priority, trade-offs, accountability, conditions for investment | Confirm who can approve this purchase and which committee or delegation limits apply; sponsorship alone is insufficient |
| Budget owner / finance / FP&A | Funding source, planning cycle, cash commitments, alternatives competing for funds | Confirm available funding, approved business case, and who can release it |
| Platform / engineering leadership | Standardisation, operational burden, delivery ownership, developer impact | Confirm scope and influence across teams, plus authority to commit technical resources |
| Architecture / enterprise IT | Integration, hosting, identity, infrastructure ownership and constraints | Confirm the architecture-review path and mandatory acceptance conditions |
| AppSec / CISO / risk | Required controls, residual risk, evidence, exception authority | Confirm who reviews evidence, accepts exceptions, and can stop progression |
| Procurement / vendor management | Supplier onboarding, purchasing route, existing contracts, purchase order | Confirm steps, owners, dependencies and lead times; procurement is not automatically the economic buyer |
| Legal / privacy reviewers | Required contract and data-handling review, acceptable terms, unresolved issues | Confirm who must review and who has signing authority; provide no legal conclusion or assumed standard approval time |
| Developers / service owners / adoption lead | Workflow continuity, training, support, practical adoption criteria | Confirm acceptance and operational ownership after purchase, not just interest in a demo |
| Partner / reseller / implementation adviser, if relevant | Influence on solution selection, contracting route and delivery ownership | Validate the buyer's actual partner relationships; do not invent an involved partner |

One person may occupy several roles, and authority may be distributed. Champion, supporter, technical approver, budget holder, and authorised signer should remain distinct labels until evidence supports combining them. The public [MEDDPPICC handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/meddppicc/) provides the qualification vocabulary; the role hypotheses above are not assertions about a real account.

## 2. Stakeholder relationships and champion evidence

Propose a map with **role, business outcome, influence, approval authority, current stance, relationship/access, evidence source/date, confidence, and next agreed action**. Leave names and unconfirmed relationships blank. Separate direct buyer statements from second-hand information and seller hypotheses.

Test a potential champion through observable, permission-based actions: helping involve the right decision participants, checking the internal approval route, and co-authoring an accurate case for change. A friendly contact or enthusiastic user is not automatically a champion. If the relationship is single-threaded, record the risk and seek agreed access rather than going around the contact. GitLab's [opportunity guidance](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/sales-operating-procedures/facilitate-the-opportunity/) distinguishes champion influence and technical versus economic commitment.

Include one fictional disagreement: platform values workflow standardisation, security needs evidence governance, and finance cannot yet confirm funding. Show how each concern changes the evaluation or next action without claiming any person has actually agreed.

## 3. Buying process: evidence gates, not a promised close date

This is a proposed buyer-side map, not GitLab's official CRM stage definitions. Some reviews may run in parallel; validate sequence and ownership with the buyer.

| Proposed gate | Evidence / exit condition | Risk if missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognise and prioritise the problem | Affected teams and accountable sponsor validate the problem and competing priorities | An interesting tool discussion without a funded initiative |
| Agree decision participants and criteria | Known approvers, technical/business criteria, acceptable alternatives, and decision method | A favourable demo with an incomplete buying group |
| Complete technical evaluation | Buyer accepts results against the existing Lab 02 scorecard; document remaining conditions | Lab 01's passing demo is mistaken for the buyer's technical acceptance |
| Review architecture, security and privacy | Required reviewers accept evidence or identify owned, dated exceptions | Hidden veto or late review; a scanner pass cannot substitute for organisational approval |
| Obtain economic approval | Authorised buyer accepts the business case and finance confirms funding | Technical success is incorrectly forecast as a purchase |
| Complete the paper process | Agreed contract-review route, supplier onboarding, purchasing requirements, authorised signer and any PO dependencies | Verbal support without an executable order |
| Confirm purchase and success handoff | Approved purchasing evidence and agreed adoption/success ownership | A signature is confused with adoption or realised value |

Keep **decision criteria**, **decision process**, and **paper process** separate. GitLab's [MEDDPPICC page](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/meddppicc/) treats these as distinct qualification areas. Use a mutual plan with buyer-confirmed actions, owners, dependencies, dates and evidence; unknown dates remain unknown. GitLab's [deal-closure guidance](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/sales-operating-procedures/deal-closure/) provides context for joint planning through approval and signature. No contract, purchase, or deployment is executed in this portfolio.

## 4. Seller-team orchestration and opportunity judgement

Explain when an account executive would ask for a solutions architect's technical help, coordinate with procurement/partner specialists, and prepare a customer-success handoff. GitLab's [enterprise sales handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/sales/playbook/) describes work across the deal team. This is role-play planning, not a claim that those GitLab teams have engaged with this project.

Add a short evidence-led deal review: what is confirmed, what is assumed, who is missing, which dependency threatens progress, and the next mutually agreed action. Include loss of a champion, delayed security review, unconfirmed budget, and a retain-current-platform outcome as scenarios. Do not assign a forecast probability, invent urgency, or label an opportunity committed because a pilot passes.
